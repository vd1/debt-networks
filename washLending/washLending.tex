\begin{abstract}
A vault that lends against its own shares creates an interest-rebate
loop: borrowers pay interest to the vault, then recover part of that
interest through their vault shares.  This lowers their effective borrow
cost, creates self-reinforcing leverage, and can make borrower demand
non-monotone in the borrow rate.  The note derives the mechanism first
in a two-player example, then in a heterogeneous-borrower equilibrium,
and closes with a tranche interpretation of the resulting risk transfer.
\end{abstract}

We are given a vault $V$ where players can deposit a stable $S$ (e.g.\ USDC) 
for a share token $X$ (e.g.\ syrupUSDC).
The vault's strategy is to direct the $S$s it receives to two venues:
\begin{itemize}
  \item half goes to another vault $L$ that generates $s = 8\%$ APY (e.g.\ a leveraged carry position on LSTs)
  \item half goes into an $X/S$ lending market $M$ where people can deposit-$X$-and-borrow-$S$ at $b = 3\%$ borrow rate
\end{itemize}

Now suppose the following scenario with two players $P_1$ and $P_2$.
Start with an empty vault.

\subsection*{Step 1}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_1$ deposits 100 $S$ in $V$ for 100 $X$
  \item $P_2$ deposits 100 $S$ in $V$ for 100 $X$
  \item $V$ posts 100 $S$ on $M$
  \item $V$ posts 100 $S$ on $L$
\end{itemize}

Positions:
\begin{align*}
  P_1 &= 100\; X \\
  P_2 &= 100\; X \\
  V   &= 100\; S \text{ (0 lent) on } M@3\% \;+\; 100\; S \text{ on } L@8\%
\end{align*}

Yields:
\begin{align*}
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } L &= 100 \times 0.08 = 8 \\
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } M &= 0 \times 0.03 = 0 \\
  \text{yield}(V) &= 8 \\
  \text{yield}(P_1) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8 = 4 \\
  \text{yield}(P_2) &= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 8 = 4
\end{align*}

\subsection*{Step 2}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_2$ deposits 100 $X$ as collateral on $M$
  \item $P_2$ borrows 60 $S$ on $M$ \emph{(entirely provided by $V$)}
  \item $P_2$ deposits 60 $S$ in $V$ for 60 $X$
  \item $V$ posts 30 $S$ on $M$
  \item $V$ posts 30 $S$ on $L$
\end{itemize}

Positions:
\begin{align*}
  P_1 &= 100\; X \\
  P_2 &= 160\; X - 60\; S \\
  V   &= 130\; S \text{ (60 lent) on } M@3\% \;+\; 130\; S \text{ on } L@8\%
\end{align*}

Yields:
\begin{align*}
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } L &= 130 \times 0.08 = 10.4 \quad \text{(\emph{external yield})} \\
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } M &= 60 \times 0.03 = 1.8 \\
  \text{yield}(V) &= 12.2 \\
  \text{yield}(P_1) &= \tfrac{100}{260} \times 12.2 = 4.69 \\
  \text{yield}(P_2) &= \tfrac{160}{260} \times 12.2 - 1.8 = 5.71
\end{align*}

At that stage we can make a number of remarks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $V$'s strategy was to put everything on $L$ at step~1, $\text{yield}(V) = 200 \times 0.08 = 16$, so $V$ is leaving money on the table.
  \item By looping its position, $P_2$ gets a higher share of the common yield, i.e.\ $\text{yield}(P_1) < \text{yield}(P_2)$.
  \item $P_2$ has changed that somewhat, now $V$ is putting $+30$ on $L$.
  \item $P_2$ pays 1.8 to borrow but gets back $\frac{16}{26}$ of that, so effective $b_{\text{eff}} = 1.15\% < b$ paid to the rest of the players $P_1$.
  \item $\text{totalX}_{\text{in}V}/\text{totalS}_{\text{in}V} = 260/260 = 1$.
  \item $\text{yield}(V) > \text{yield}(P_1) + \text{yield}(P_2) = \text{external yield } 10.4$, which equals the external $L$ yield; the 1.8 from $M$ is an internal transfer from $P_2$ to all players.
  \item $P_2$ has more risk (liquidation), so one can see the premium as a reward for that risk.
  \item Withdrawals from $V$ are fine as long as $M$ is liquid: right now $P_1$ can exit because $V$ has 200 instant $S$ at disposal, if $P_1$ leaves\ldots
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Step 3}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_1$ withdraws 100 $X$
  \item $V$ pulls 50 $S$ from $M$
  \item $V$ pulls 50 $S$ from $L$
\end{itemize}

Positions:
\begin{align*}
  P_1 &= 0\; X \\
  P_2 &= 160\; X - 60\; S \\
  V   &= 80\; S \text{ (60 lent) on } M@3\% \;+\; 80\; S \text{ on } L@8\%
\end{align*}

Yields:
\begin{align*}
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } L &= 80 \times 0.08 = 6.4 \\
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } M &= 60 \times 0.03 = 1.8 \quad \text{(\emph{assuming constant rate})} \\
  \text{yield}(V) &= 8.2 \\
  \text{yield}(P_1) &= 0 \\
  \text{yield}(P_2) &= \tfrac{160}{160} \times 8.2 - 1.8 = 6.4
\end{align*}

Note that $P_2$'s LTV $y_2/x_2$ is unchanged.

\section{Second attempt}

Assume fractions $\varepsilon + \varepsilon' = 1$.
Let rates be $b$ (borrow on $M$) and $s$ (APY on $L$).

\subsection*{Step 1}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_1$ deposits $x_1$ $S$ in $V$ for $x_1$ shares $X$
  \item $P_2$ deposits $x_2$ $S$ in $V$ for $x_2$ shares $X$
  \item $V$ posts $\varepsilon(x_1+x_2)$ $S$ on $L$@$s$
  \item $V$ posts $\varepsilon'(x_1+x_2)$ $S$ on $M$@$b$
\end{itemize}

Yields:
\begin{align*}
  \text{yield}(V) \text{ on } L &= s\varepsilon(x_1+x_2) \\
  \text{yield}(P_1) &= x_1 s\varepsilon \\
  \text{yield}(P_2) &= x_2 s\varepsilon
\end{align*}

\subsection*{Step 2}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_2$ posts $x_2$ $X$ as collateral in $M$
  \item $P_2$ borrows $y_2 < \varepsilon'(x_1+x_2)$ on $M$ (liquidity constraint; also needs $y_2/x_2$ lower than maxLTV)
  \item $P_2$ deposits $y_2$ $S$ in $V$ for $y_2$ $X$ (or has the rate changed?)
  \item $V$ posts $\varepsilon y_2$ $S$ on $L$@$s$
  \item $V$ posts $\varepsilon' y_2$ $S$ on $M$@$b$
\end{itemize}

Yields: write $T = x_1 + x_2 + y_2$ for the amount of $S$ in $V$ after step 2.
\begin{align*}
  \text{yield}(V) &= s\varepsilon T + b\, y_2 \\
  \text{yield}(P_1) &= \frac{x_1}{T}\bigl(s\varepsilon T + b\, y_2\bigr) \\
  \text{yield}(P_2) &= \frac{x_2+y_2}{T}\bigl(s\varepsilon T + b\, y_2\bigr) - b\, y_2
    = (x_2+y_2)\,s\varepsilon - b_{\text{eff}}\, y_2
\end{align*}
with:
\[
  b_{\text{eff}} = \frac{x_1}{T}\, b
\]
the effective rate at which $P_2$ is borrowing.

\medskip\noindent
Players' net equals external yield:
\[
  \text{yield}(P_1) + \text{yield}(P_2) = s\varepsilon T
\]

\noindent
Players' incremental gains from looping $y_2$ (vs.\ no loop):
\begin{align*}
  \Delta_1 &= \frac{x_1}{T}\bigl(s\varepsilon T + b\, y_2\bigr) - x_1 s\varepsilon
            = \frac{x_1}{T}\, b\, y_2 \geq 0 \\
  \Delta_2 &= (x_2+y_2)\,s\varepsilon - \frac{x_1}{T}\, b\, y_2 - x_2 s\varepsilon
            = y_2\Bigl(s\varepsilon - \frac{x_1}{T}\, b\Bigr)
\end{align*}

The loop is attractive to $P_2$ if $\varepsilon s > \frac{x_1}{T}\, b$. It may be that $s < b$ (no carry) and yet $\varepsilon s > \frac{x_1}{T}\, b$ (some 
notion of effective carry).

\subsection*{Step 3}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $P_1$ pushes $y_1$ $S$ from $V$ with $y_1 \in [-x_1, +\infty)$
  \item $V$ pushes $\varepsilon y_1$ to $L$
  \item $V$ pushes $\varepsilon' y_1$ to $M$
\end{itemize}

Yields: write $T' = x_1 + y_1 + x_2 + y_2$ for the new amount of $S$ in $V$.
\begin{align*}
  \text{yield}(V) &= s\varepsilon T' + b\, y_2 \\
  \text{yield}(P_1) &= \frac{x_1+y_1}{T'}\bigl(s\varepsilon T' + b\, y_2\bigr) \\
  \text{yield}(P_2) &= \frac{x_2+y_2}{T'}\bigl(s\varepsilon T' + b\, y_2\bigr) - b\, y_2
    = (x_2+y_2)\,s\varepsilon - \frac{x_1+y_1}{T'}\, b\, y_2
\end{align*}

If $P_1$ withdraws everything, $x_1+y_1=0$ and $\text{yield}(P_2) = (x_2+y_2)\,s\varepsilon$, so the effective borrowing rate drops to zero (in which case $P_2$ can redirect the vault allocation to $L$ without $V$ noticing, so to speak---$P_2$ is a rate maker). If to the contrary $P_1$ takes a very large position, $P_2$ is negligible and its rate goes to $b$ ($P_2$ is rate taker).

\section{Heterogeneous borrowers with fixed loanable supply}

We now place the wash-lending loop of the previous section inside the
borrower equilibrium framework of
\texttt{morpho\_borrower\_equilibrium.tex}.  There are $n$ borrowers,
each characterised by equity $e_i$, maximum multiplier $M_i$
(risk tolerance), and minimum acceptable APY $a_i$.  Write
$E = \sum_{j=1}^n e_j$ for total equity.

The vault $V$ allocates fraction $\varepsilon$ to external yield $L$
at rate $s$ and fraction $\varepsilon' = 1-\varepsilon$ to market $M$.

$\blacktriangleright$ The loanable supply on $M$ is fixed at $\bar S$.

\subsection*{The loop}

Borrower $i$ deposits $e_i$ of $S$ in $V$ for $e_i$ shares $X$, posts
$X$ as collateral in $M$, borrows $y_i = m_i e_i$ of $S$, and
re-deposits in $V$.  After all loops:
\[
  T = E + Y, \qquad Y = \sum_j m_j e_j, \qquad
  \varphi = \frac{Y}{T}.
\]

\subsection*{Modified APY}

Vault income is $s\varepsilon T + rY$.  Borrower $i$'s pro-rata share
of vault income minus interest cost gives APY on equity:
\[
  A_i
  = (1+m_i)\,s\varepsilon + r\,\frac{Y - m_i E}{E+Y}
  = \underbrace{(s\varepsilon + r\varphi)}_{c_v}
    + m_i\underbrace{\bigl(s\varepsilon - r(1-\varphi)\bigr)}_{\Delta_v}.
\]
This has the Morpho form $A = c + m\Delta$, but both the collateral
yield $c_v = s\varepsilon + r\varphi$ and the carry
$\Delta_v = s\varepsilon - r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ are \emph{endogenous}:
they depend on the borrow rate $r$ and aggregate debt $Y$ through
\[
  r_{\mathrm{eff}} = r(1-\varphi) = \frac{rE}{E+Y}.
\]
This is the effective borrow cost after the vault rebate (cf.\
$b_{\mathrm{eff}} = \frac{x_1}{T}\,b$ from Section~2).

\subsection*{Bang-bang}

Since $A_i$ is linear in $m_i$, the price-taking optimum is still
bang-bang: $m_i = M_i$ if $A_i(M_i) \ge a_i$, else $m_i = 0$.

\subsection*{Participation condition (at $Y = \bar S$)}

Assume the market clears: $Y = \bar S$, so $T = E + \bar S$ and
$\varphi = \bar S/(E+\bar S)$.  The condition
$A_i(M_i) \ge a_i$ becomes
\[
  s\varepsilon(1+M_i) + r\,\frac{\bar S - M_i E}{E+\bar S}
  \;\ge\; a_i.
\]
Define $\beta_i := (\bar S - M_i E)/(E+\bar S)$ and
$\delta_i := a_i - s\varepsilon(1+M_i)$.  The sign of $\beta_i$
(equivalently, whether $M_i \lessgtr \bar S/E$) partitions borrowers
into four types:

\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Rate sensitivity}
  & \textbf{Threshold} \\[4pt]
A & $M_i < \bar S/E$, $\delta_i \le 0$
  & always active; higher $r$ helps & --- \\
B & $M_i < \bar S/E$, $\delta_i > 0$
  & active iff $r \ge \tilde r_i := \delta_i/\beta_i$
  & floor rate \\
C & $M_i > \bar S/E$, $\delta_i \le 0$
  & active iff $r \le \tilde\rho_i := -\delta_i/(-\beta_i)$
  & ceiling rate \\
D & $M_i > \bar S/E$, $\delta_i > 0$
  & never active & ---
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip\noindent
The boundary $M_i = \bar S/E$ is the \emph{rate-neutral} multiplier:
at that leverage, the borrower recaptures exactly the interest it pays
through the vault.

\subsection*{Demand curve}

Aggregate demand at rate $r$:
\[
  D(r) = \underbrace{\sum_{i \in A} e_i M_i}_{D_A}
       + \underbrace{\sum_{\substack{i \in B \\ r \ge \tilde r_i}}
           e_i M_i}_{D_B(r)}
       + \underbrace{\sum_{\substack{i \in C \\ r \le \tilde\rho_i}}
           e_i M_i}_{D_C(r)}.
\]
$D_B(r)$ is a step function that \emph{increases} in $r$ (rate-seekers
join); $D_C(r)$ is a step function that \emph{decreases} in $r$
(standard Morpho exit).  Therefore $D(r)$ is generically
\textbf{non-monotone}.

\subsection*{Equilibrium}

The clearing rate $r^*$ satisfies $D(r^*) = \bar S$, with the marginal
borrower partially filled if $D$ jumps over $\bar S$ at a step.  Three
regimes:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $D(0) > \bar S$: excess demand at $r=0$ from Types A+C.
    The rate rises, Type~C borrowers exit.  Clearing lies among
    Type~C thresholds $\tilde\rho_i$ (standard regime).
  \item $D(0) < \bar S < D(\infty)$: insufficient demand at low rates.
    The rate must \emph{rise} to attract Type~B demand.  Clearing
    lies among Type~B thresholds $\tilde r_i$ (inverted regime).
  \item The upward (B) and downward (C) steps interleave: $D(r)$
    crosses $\bar S$ at multiple values of $r$.  \textbf{Multiple
    equilibria} are possible.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Qualitative differences from the standard model}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Self-reinforcing leverage.}  As $Y$ rises, $\varphi$
    rises, $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ falls.  Each borrower's entry makes
    others more profitable---a positive externality absent from the
    standard model.

  \item \textbf{Inverted rate sensitivity.}  Modest-leverage borrowers
    ($M_i < \bar S/E$) are \emph{better off} when rates are high,
    because they recapture more interest through the vault than they
    pay.

  \item \textbf{Multiple equilibria.}  The fixed-point
    $Y^* = G(Y^*)$, where $G$ maps aggregate debt to consistent
    demand, can have multiple crossings.  A high-$Y$ equilibrium
    with strong rebate can coexist with a low-$Y$ equilibrium with
    weak rebate.

  \item \textbf{Amplified predatory entry.}  From
    \texttt{morpho\_borrower\_equilibrium.tex} \S4: a large entrant
    profits from knocking out incumbents (their exit reduces aggregate
    debt, lowering the terminal rate).  In the wash-lending version
    this compounds: the entrant's debt also increases $\varphi$,
    boosting the vault rebate.  The APY gain from a cascade of $k$
    exits is
    \[
      A_0^{(k)} - \hat A_0
      = m_0\kappa\sum_{i=1}^k d_i
        + (1+m_0)\,r^*\,
          \frac{Y_{\mathrm{post}} - Y_{\mathrm{pre}}}
               {(E+Y_{\mathrm{post}})(E+Y_{\mathrm{pre}})}\,E,
    \]
    where the first term is the standard rate-relief channel (linear
    model, $r(Y) = r_b + \kappa Y$) and the second is the rebate
    channel from the vault loop.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Application to the Resolv USR incident}

USR is the stable $S$; the Resolv vault is $V$; the compromised mint
authority is equivalent to an unbounded shock $\varepsilon' \to 1$,
redirecting all backing to lending with $s\varepsilon \to 0$.  In that
limit the carry inverts:
\[
  \Delta_v = s\varepsilon - r_{\mathrm{eff}}
  \;\longrightarrow\; -r_{\mathrm{eff}} < 0.
\]
Every borrower faces negative carry; the bang-bang optimum flips from
$m_i = M_i$ to $m_i = 0$.  The self-reinforcing leverage that
sustained the high-$Y$ equilibrium becomes a self-reinforcing
\emph{unwind}: each exit reduces $\varphi$, raising $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$
for survivors, knocking out the next marginal borrower.  The cascade
terminates only when $Y = 0$ (full deleveraging) or when the rate
drops to a level consistent with the remaining active set---but with
$s\varepsilon = 0$ no such interior fixed point exists.

This is the blast radius of a mint-authority compromise expressed in
the borrower equilibrium language: the entire active set is expelled
in a single cascade, and the welfare loss equals
$\sum_{i=1}^n e_i\bigl[c_v + M_i \Delta_v\bigr]$ evaluated at the
pre-hack equilibrium.

\subsection*{Implicit tranching}

The wash-lending equilibrium admits a structured-finance interpretation
that clarifies the risk allocation.  Partition participants into two
roles:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Senior tranche (lenders).}  Players who deposit $S$ in
    $V$ but do \emph{not} loop.  Their yield is $s\varepsilon$---the
    vault's external rate, independent of the borrow market.  They bear
    no liquidation risk and are first in line for the vault's assets.

  \item \textbf{Junior tranche (loopers).}  Players who loop $S$ back
    through $M$.  Their yield is $c_v + M_i \Delta_v$, which exceeds
    the senior yield when $\Delta_v > 0$ (positive carry) but falls
    below it---and can go negative---when rates spike or the backing
    collapses.  They are levered, bear liquidation risk, and absorb
    first losses.
\end{itemize}

The borrow rate $r^*$ is the endogenous price of risk transfer between
the two tranches: it determines both the senior coupon
$s\varepsilon + r^*\varphi$ (boosted by interest income) and the junior
spread $\Delta_v = s\varepsilon - r^*(1-\varphi)$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Bifurcation of the tranche split.}  The multiple equilibria
from the clearing analysis above acquire a direct structural
interpretation: at the \emph{same} pool configuration (same $E$,
same $\bar S$, same borrower population), the market can settle into

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \textbf{thin junior} equilibrium: low $Y$, weak rebate,
    high effective borrow cost $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$---only the most
    risk-tolerant borrowers lever up, and most capital sits in the
    senior tranche earning $\approx s\varepsilon$;

  \item a \textbf{thick junior} equilibrium: high $Y$, strong rebate,
    low $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$---the self-reinforcing leverage channel
    pulls in modest-leverage borrowers (Type~B), the junior tranche
    dominates the capital structure, and the senior tranche earns a
    higher coupon from the large interest flow.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The transition between these states is discontinuous: a small shock to
$\bar S$ or $s$ can flip the pool from one regime to the other,
producing a sudden reallocation of risk that looks like a run on the
junior tranche (borrowers exit en masse) or a leverage spiral (new
borrowers pile in).

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Comparison with traditional CDOs.}  In a standard CDO the
tranche boundaries (attachment/detachment points) are fixed at
issuance.  Here they are \emph{endogenous}: the size of the junior
tranche ($Y$) and its compensation ($\Delta_v$) are equilibrium
objects determined by the borrower population and the vault's
allocation rule $\varepsilon/\varepsilon'$.  Governance changes to
$\varepsilon$ (e.g.\ Sky adjusting the DSR or the Spark allocation)
are analogous to restructuring the waterfall mid-life---something
impossible in traditional structured finance but routine in DeFi.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Application.}  Spark/Sky is the canonical example:
DAI lenders are senior (yield = DSR $\approx s$), DAI
borrowers who loop through Spark are junior (leveraged carry on
collateral yield minus effective borrow cost).  The DSR is the
governance lever that reprices the tranche split---raising it
simultaneously increases senior yield and compresses junior carry,
potentially triggering the thin-junior equilibrium.

\subsection*{Tranche attachment points}

The analogy with structured finance becomes quantitative once we
compute the collateral-loss threshold at which junior is wiped out and
losses begin hitting senior.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Setup.}  At equilibrium the junior tranche (loopers) holds
aggregate collateral value
\[
  X_J = \sum_{i \in \text{active}} (1+M_i)\,e_i = E_J + Y,
\]
where $E_J = \sum_{\text{active}} e_i$ is junior equity and $Y = \bar S$
is total debt.  The senior tranche holds $E_S = E - E_J$ (non-looping
deposits).  Total vault assets are $T = E + Y$ in $S$-terms, backed by
collateral worth $X_J$ at current prices plus the vault's external
position $\varepsilon T$ on $L$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Collateral shock.}  Suppose the collateral price $p_X$ drops
by a fraction $\delta$, so collateral is worth $(1-\delta)\,X_J$.
Borrower $i$ is liquidated when their LTV exceeds the liquidation
threshold $\ell$:
\[
  \frac{m_i\,e_i}{(1-\delta)(1+m_i)\,e_i} > \ell
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \delta > 1 - \frac{m_i}{\ell\,(1+m_i)}.
\]
Define the \emph{individual liquidation threshold}
\[
  \delta_i^* := 1 - \frac{m_i}{\ell\,(1+m_i)}
  = 1 - \frac{M_i}{\ell\,(1+M_i)}
\]
(using $m_i = M_i$ at the bang-bang equilibrium).  Borrowers with
higher leverage are liquidated first: $\delta_i^*$ is decreasing in
$M_i$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Junior wipeout (attachment point).}  As $\delta$ increases
past each $\delta_i^*$, the corresponding borrower is liquidated.
If liquidation recovers $(1-\delta)(1+M_i)\,e_i$ against debt
$M_i\,e_i$, the shortfall per borrower is
\[
  L_i(\delta) = \max\bigl(0,\; M_i\,e_i - (1-\delta)(1+M_i)\,e_i\bigr)
  = e_i\,\max\bigl(0,\; \delta(1+M_i) - 1\bigr).
\]
The junior tranche absorbs losses up to its total equity $E_J$.
The \textbf{attachment point} $\delta_A$ is the smallest shock at
which cumulative losses exhaust junior equity:
\[
  \sum_{i \in \text{active}} L_i(\delta_A) = E_J.
\]
For $\delta > \delta_A$, losses spill into the senior tranche (vault
depositors who did not loop).

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Closed form (homogeneous case).}  If all active borrowers
have the same multiplier $M$, then $E_J = Y/M$, $X_J = (1+M)\,E_J$,
and the attachment point simplifies to
\[
  \boxed{\delta_A = \frac{1}{1+M}\,\bigl(1 + M\,(1-\ell)\bigr)
  = 1 - \frac{M\,\ell}{1+M}.}
\]
At Spark-like parameters ($\ell = 0.83$, $M = 4$):
$\delta_A = 1 - \frac{4 \times 0.83}{5} = 1 - 0.664 = 0.336$,
meaning a 33.6\% collateral drop wipes out junior.  At Morpho's
high-LLTV regime ($\ell = 0.945$, $M = 15$):
$\delta_A = 1 - \frac{15 \times 0.945}{16} = 1 - 0.886 = 0.114$,
only 11.4\%.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Detachment point.}  The \textbf{detachment point} $\delta_D$
is the shock at which total vault losses equal $E_J + E_S = E$
(both tranches wiped, only debt remains).  In the homogeneous case:
\[
  \delta_D = 1.
\]
Since collateral can't go below zero, $\delta_D = 1$ always---but the
senior tranche's loss grows linearly for $\delta \in [\delta_A, 1]$.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Endogenous attachment.}  Unlike a fixed CDO, $\delta_A$
depends on the equilibrium leverage $M^*$ (or the distribution of
$M_i$'s in the heterogeneous case), which itself depends on $\bar S$,
$s$, $\varepsilon$, and the borrower population.  A governance change
that shifts the equilibrium from thin-junior to thick-junior
\emph{simultaneously lowers the attachment point}---more leverage
means the loss-absorption buffer shrinks.  This is the quantitative
content of the ``endogenous CDO'' analogy: the waterfall reshapes
itself in response to market conditions and governance actions.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Volatility-adjusted loss probability.}  If collateral returns
are normally distributed with annualized volatility $\sigma$ and the
position horizon is $\tau$ (e.g.\ the time to liquidation oracle
update), the probability of hitting the attachment point is
\[
  \Pr[\delta > \delta_A]
  = \Phi\!\left(\frac{-\delta_A}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}\right),
\]
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF.  

For ETH ($\sigma \approx
0.80$) over a 1-day horizon ($\tau = 1/365$), the probability of
a 33.6\% drop is negligible; over a 7-day horizon it is still small
but non-trivial for tail events.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Choice of horizon $\tau$.}
The horizon $\tau$ should reflect the lender's \emph{worst-case time to
exit} under the stress scenario being priced: $\tau = \tau_{\mathrm{detect}}
+ \tau_{\mathrm{exit}}$, where $\tau_{\mathrm{detect}}$ is the delay
before the lender recognises the drawdown, and $\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}$ is
the time needed to withdraw supply from the pool.
On Morpho Blue, $\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}$ depends on available liquidity:
if utilisation is high, only a small fraction of supply can be redeemed
instantly, and a depeg event triggers correlated withdrawals that
further reduce liquidity---a bank-run dynamic that makes
$\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}$ \emph{endogenous} to the crisis.
Crucially, the sensitivity of the fair spread to $\tau$ is highly
nonlinear and market-dependent.  For thick-buffer markets ($\delta_A
\gtrsim 50\%$, e.g.\ ETH/BTC-collateral pools), increasing $\tau$
from 7 to 30\,days barely moves the breach probability, because
$\delta_A/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$ remains deep in the tail.  For
thin-buffer stablecoin loops ($\delta_A \approx 5$--$15\%$), the same
change can push the breach probability from negligible to order-one,
causing the fair spread to explode.  A single global $\tau$ therefore
masks systematic underpricing of the most fragile markets; the
disciplined approach is to assign a per-market or per-asset-class
horizon---e.g.\ $\tau = 7\,\text{d}$ for liquid crypto-collateral
pools and $\tau = 30\,\text{d}$ for stablecoin loops where exit
liquidity evaporates precisely when it is most needed.

\texttt{wash\_tranche.py} plots the full waterfall and loss
distributions for various parameter regimes.

\section{Revision notes}

These are editorial and mathematical notes to incorporate in a later
revision.  They are intentionally appended rather than folded into the
main text, so the current argument remains unchanged.

\subsection*{1. Collateral convention}

The current draft mixes two different looping conventions.
In the initial numerical example, $P_2$ starts with $100X$, posts that
$100X$ as collateral, borrows $60S$, and deposits the borrowed $60S$
back into $V$ for another $60X$.  The balance sheet is then written as
\[
  P_2 = 160X - 60S.
\]
That is economically correct as a net position, but it is ambiguous as a
lending-market position.  Did $P_2$ pledge only the first $100X$, or did
$P_2$ also pledge the newly minted $60X$?

If only the first $100X$ is collateral, the lending-market LTV is
\[
  \frac{60}{100} = 60\%.
\]
If the full $160X$ is collateral, the lending-market LTV is
\[
  \frac{60}{160} = 37.5\%.
\]
These are different models.

The later tranche and TVL sections use the recursive-loop convention:
initial equity is $e_i$, debt is $m_i e_i$, and total vault
shares/collateral after looping are $(1+m_i)e_i$.  Under that convention
the lending-market LTV is
\[
  \frac{m_i e_i}{(1+m_i)e_i} = \frac{m_i}{1+m_i},
\]
so the LLTV constraint is
\[
  \frac{m_i}{1+m_i} \leq \ell
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  m_i \leq \frac{\ell}{1-\ell}.
\]

The paper should choose one convention and carry it throughout.  Since
the final section on Paul's tweet uses the recursive convention, the
cleanest revision is probably to rewrite the earlier examples in that
language:
\begin{align*}
  e_i &= \text{initial equity}, \\
  m_i e_i &= \text{borrowed amount}, \\
  (1+m_i)e_i &= \text{total collateral/shares after looping}.
\end{align*}
This avoids using $M$ sometimes as borrow-against-original-equity and
sometimes as recursive debt/equity multiplier.

\subsection*{2. Senior yield statement}

The current tranche interpretation says that non-looping depositors earn
$s\varepsilon$ and that their yield is independent of the borrow market.
That is not consistent with the earlier model.  The heterogeneous
borrower section derives
\[
  A_i = c_v + m_i\Delta_v,
  \qquad
  c_v = s\varepsilon + r\varphi,
  \qquad
  \Delta_v = s\varepsilon - r(1-\varphi).
\]
A non-looper has $m_i=0$, hence
\[
  A_i = c_v = s\varepsilon + r\varphi.
\]

So senior/non-looping depositors do receive borrow-market income through
the vault.  They do not only receive the external $L$ yield.  The actual
distinction is:
\[
  \text{senior/non-looper yield} = c_v,
\]
while
\[
  \text{junior/looper yield} = c_v + m_i\Delta_v.
\]
Both groups receive the base vault return $c_v$.  Loopers additionally
pay borrowing costs and lever the base return, so their incremental
economics are governed by
\[
  \Delta_v = s\varepsilon - r(1-\varphi).
\]
That is the more precise tranche split: seniors get the base vault
return; juniors lever it and are exposed to whether incremental carry is
positive or negative.

\subsection*{3. Attachment-point math}

The attachment-point section needs a careful rewrite.  It currently
defines
\[
  L_i(\delta)
  = \max\bigl(0,\; M_i e_i - (1-\delta)(1+M_i)e_i\bigr).
\]
This is not the loss absorbed by the junior tranche.  It is the
lender/senior shortfall after collateral value has already fallen below
debt.  In other words, it starts counting losses after junior equity is
gone.

For a homogeneous recursive loop, let
\[
  \text{collateral} = (1+M)e, \qquad
  \text{debt} = Me.
\]
After a collateral price drop $\delta$, collateral is worth
\[
  (1-\delta)(1+M)e.
\]
The looper's remaining equity is
\[
  (1-\delta)(1+M)e - Me
  = \bigl[1-\delta(1+M)\bigr]e.
\]
Junior equity is wiped when this equals zero:
\[
  1-\delta(1+M)=0
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \delta_A = \frac{1}{1+M}.
\]
Thus if $M=4$, junior is wiped at $1/5 = 20\%$.  If $M=15$, junior is
wiped at $1/16 = 6.25\%$.  These are the clean attachment-point numbers
when attachment means ``senior losses begin'' absent liquidation.

The liquidation threshold is separate.  If the liquidation LLTV is
$\ell$, liquidation occurs when
\[
  \frac{Me}{(1-\delta)(1+M)e} > \ell.
\]
Solving gives
\[
  \delta_{\mathrm{liq}}
  = 1 - \frac{M}{\ell(1+M)}.
\]
For $\ell=0.83$ and $M=4$,
\[
  \delta_{\mathrm{liq}}
  = 1 - \frac{4}{0.83 \cdot 5}
  \approx 3.6\%.
\]
Liquidation therefore triggers long before junior equity is fully wiped,
which is the intended function of liquidation.

The current formula
\[
  1 - \frac{M\ell}{1+M}
\]
is neither the liquidation trigger nor the equity wipeout point under the
recursive definitions above.  A cleaner revision should separate:
\[
  \delta_{\mathrm{liq}} = 1-\frac{M}{\ell(1+M)}
  \quad\text{(liquidation starts),}
\]
\[
  \delta_A = \frac{1}{1+M}
  \quad\text{(junior equity is gone and senior loss begins absent liquidation),}
\]
and
\[
  L_{\mathrm{senior}}(\delta)
  = \max\bigl(0,\; Me-(1-\delta)(1+M)e\bigr)
  \quad\text{(senior shortfall after attachment).}
\]

\subsection*{4. Fixed loanable supply versus vault allocation}

The heterogeneous section currently combines two assumptions that are
not automatically compatible.  First, the vault allocates a fixed
fraction $\varepsilon$ to $L$ and $\varepsilon'=1-\varepsilon$ to $M$.
Second, the loanable supply on $M$ is taken to be fixed at $\bar S$, and
the clearing condition is written as
\[
  Y = \bar S.
\]
If $M$ supply comes from the vault's allocation rule, then the supply is
not exogenous.  It is
\[
  S_M(Y) = \varepsilon'(E+Y).
\]
If all supplied funds are borrowed at clearing, then
\[
  Y = \varepsilon'(E+Y),
\]
so
\[
  Y(1-\varepsilon') = \varepsilon'E
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  Y = \frac{\varepsilon'}{\varepsilon}E.
\]

The model should therefore choose one of two clean formulations.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Fixed external supply.}
The lending market has exogenous loanable supply $\bar S$.  The vault may
own some or all of that supply, but $\bar S$ is treated as fixed for the
equilibrium exercise.  In that case the fixed-fraction allocation
language should be softened in this section.

\medskip\noindent
\textbf{Endogenous vault supply.}
Loanable supply is
\[
  S_M(Y) = \varepsilon'(E+Y),
\]
and borrowing must satisfy
\[
  Y \leq S_M(Y).
\]
Then clearing is a fixed point involving both borrower demand and the
vault allocation rule.  This is more faithful to the wash-lending loop,
but it is also more involved.

\subsection*{5. Price-taking versus strategic loopers}

The bang-bang result is correct as a price-taking benchmark, but the
assumption should be stated explicitly.  Since
\[
  A_i = c_v + m_i\Delta_v
\]
is linear in $m_i$, the optimum is $m_i=M_i$ or $m_i=0$ if borrower $i$
treats
\[
  r,\qquad \varphi,\qquad c_v,\qquad \Delta_v
\]
as fixed.

But a large looper changes aggregate debt $Y$, hence changes
\[
  \varphi = \frac{Y}{E+Y},
  \qquad
  r_{\mathrm{eff}} = r(1-\varphi),
  \qquad
  \Delta_v = s\varepsilon - r(1-\varphi).
\]
So a large looper is not simply choosing leverage at a given spread; the
looper changes the spread by looping.  This is already visible in the
two-player example: when $P_1$ exits, $P_2$ becomes the residual owner of
the vault and recaptures all interest paid to the vault, so the effective
borrow cost falls to zero.

The revision should split the analysis into:
\[
  \text{atomistic benchmark: } r \text{ and } \varphi \text{ fixed,}
\]
where the bang-bang result applies, and
\[
  \text{strategic borrower case: } m_i \text{ affects } Y,\varphi,
  \text{ and possibly } r,
\]
where the objective is nonlinear and the bang-bang conclusion does not
automatically follow.

\subsection*{6. Suggested structure}

The draft currently reads like research notes, jumping from the toy
example to the general model, the Morpho equilibrium, Resolv, the CDO
analogy, liquidation math, and Paul's tweet.  For circulation, the main
line should probably be narrower.

A clearer structure would be:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Start with the toy example.  The goal is to make the reader see
  the mechanism numerically: $P_2$ borrows from the vault, pays interest
  to the vault, and recaptures part of that interest through vault
  ownership.

  \item Generalize to the two-player model.  Derive the central identity
  \[
    b_{\mathrm{eff}} = b\frac{x_1}{T}.
  \]

  \item Move to many borrowers.  Define $E$, $Y$, and $\varphi$, then
  derive
  \[
    A_i = c_v + m_i\Delta_v.
  \]

  \item Discuss equilibrium consequences: non-monotone demand, inverted
  rate sensitivity, multiple equilibria, and the positive externality
  from aggregate leverage.

  \item Introduce the tranche interpretation as an economic analogy, not
  as literal legal seniority.

  \item Put liquidation and loss-threshold math after the tranche
  interpretation, separating liquidation trigger, junior wipeout, senior
  shortfall, and exit-liquidity horizon.

  \item Move the note on Paul's tweet and TVL inflation conventions to an
  appendix or separate closing note.
\end{enumerate}
